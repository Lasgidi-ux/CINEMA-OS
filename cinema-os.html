Beginner's Corner

Everything else on this page assumes you already know what a "key light" is. This section doesn't. Read it first. It takes about ten minutes and it will save you the six months it usually takes.

The one thing to understand before anything else

You are not "using an app". You are running a tiny film production, alone, very fast. The tools have changed what you can make. They have not changed what a film is. Everything below is just that one idea, unpacked.

What is actually happening when you type

When you describe a scene to an AI video tool, three things happen:
  1. It plans — a hidden pass that decides who is in frame, where they stand, and where the camera is.
  2. It commits — once it decides, it paints every single frame to match that decision. It cannot change its mind halfway.
  3. It invents the rest — anything you didn't specify, it decides for you, differently every time.

That third point is the whole game. Beautiful lighting and a boring story? That happens when you specified the look and left everything that matters to chance. The tool is a very talented crew with no memory, no taste, and no idea what happens next.

Six sentences, in plain language

One

You need an idea, not a shot

Before any tool, you need to know what changes. "A woman sits in a café" is not an idea. "A woman sits in a café until her ex walks in, and she pretends not to see him" is an idea. A film is about a change. Everything else is decoration.

Two

One clip is 4–10 seconds. A film is 20–200 of them.

You will never make a two-minute film in one go. You make lots of very short clips and join them. This single fact changes how you work — you are assembling, not conjuring.

Three

Make pictures first, then move them

Images are cheap, fast and free to redo. Video is expensive and slow. Get the character looking right as a photo first, then make the photo move. Skipping this step is the single biggest waste of money beginners make.

Four

The same person must look the same every time

This is called consistency, and it is the hardest problem in AI filmmaking. The fix is boring and reliable: make one excellent picture of your character, save it, and always upload that same picture when you generate them again. Never start fresh.

Five

Describe what a camera can see, not what a person feels

You cannot film "sad". You can film she looks away, swallows, and her eyes stay fixed on the table. The first version gets a blank stare. The second version gets a performance. Always describe the body, never the emotion.

Six

Sound carries more than you think

Watch any AI video on mute. If it falls apart, the picture was doing all the work. Real footage is anchored by sound — the hum of a room, footsteps, a door. Add those and suddenly it looks filmed. This is the cheapest realism available.

"Do I need to know cinematography?"

No. But you need about eight words, and you need them now rather than in six months.

That is genuinely all of it. Nobody expects you to know lighting theory. They expect you to be able to say "close up, from a low angle" instead of "make it look nice" — and honestly, even that is more than many finished films manage. The tools respond to specificity the way a nervous employee responds to a clear instruction.

Your first film: 30 seconds, eight steps

Do this once. Not for the film — for what it teaches you. Do not skip to step 5.

  1. Write one sentence with a change in it. Not a story, not a scene — one sentence. "A courier realises the package is addressed to the house she grew up in." That sentence is your whole film. Everything must serve it.
  2. Decide three things, and nothing more. Where (one location — never two for your first film). When (day or night). Who (one person, maybe two). Write them down. This is your colour bible and it's only three words long.
  3. Make your character's picture. One photo, clear face, plain background, neutral expression, standing. Generate it several times and keep the one that feels most like a real person. This is your canon. Save it somewhere permanent and never delete it.
  4. Make a picture of your location. One wide photo of the room or street. Keep it simple. This stops the background from rearranging itself between clips.
  5. Make six stills — your shot list. Don't generate any video yet. Take your six moments — wide, medium, close, plus three others — and make each one a still image. This takes ten minutes and costs nothing. Look at the six together. Are they varied? Do the cuts work? Does the story read?
  6. Turn each still into a short clip. Upload the still as your starting frame, add a short description of what should happen and where the camera goes, and generate 4–8 seconds. Draft with the cheapest model available. Generate two or three versions of each. Keep the best.
  7. Join them in your editor. Cut on movement — start the next clip while something is still moving, not after it stops. This single rule is the difference between "AI clips" and "a film."
  8. Add sound, then watch it on mute. Lay a continuous room tone under the whole 30 seconds — a hum, wind, traffic, rain, whatever belongs in your location. Add footsteps if anyone walks. Add one piece of music underneath at low volume. Then watch it with the sound off. If it still works, you've made something.
What your first film should cost

Using free tiers and image-first planning: zero to a few dollars, and roughly two to four hours of work including the learning. If you find yourself at fifty generations and no film, you skipped step 5 — you boarded the video before you boarded the shots.

The first prompt you should ever type

Not fancy. Five parts, in this order:

Camera · Subject · Action · Place · Light — plus sound
Medium shot, slow dolly in. {{who, described simply}} is
{{what they are doing}} in {{where}}.
{{where the light is coming from, and how it falls off}}.
Audio: {{the hum of the place}}, {{one physical sound}}.

Then change one thing at a time and watch what changes. Change the camera. Change the light. Change the action. If you change four things at once and it gets better, you have learned nothing — and if it gets worse, you have no idea which one did it.

Eight words and you're dangerous

You do not need film school. You need these. Everything else can be looked up when you need it.

SHOT SIZEHow much of the person you see. Wide = whole body + surroundings (lonely, small). Medium = waist up (normal conversation). Close-up = face and shoulders (emotion). Extreme close-up = just eyes or a mouth (maximum intensity).Rule of thumb: the closer you are, the more the audience feels and the less they understand.
CAMERA ANGLEEye-level = neutral, normal. Low angle = camera below, looking up — makes them powerful. High angle = camera above, looking down — makes them small or vulnerable. Over the shoulder = you're looking past one person at another."Low angle" is the single cheapest way to make an AI shot look deliberate.
CAMERA MOVEHow the camera behaves: Static/locked (doesn't move — very strong), Pan (swings side to side), Tilt (swings up/down), Dolly (moves in or out), Track (follows a person), Handheld (shaky, documentary, urgent).One move per shot. Two moves in one prompt usually gives you a confused one.
DEPTH OF FIELDHow blurry the background is. Shallow = blurry background, subject sharp — intimate, expensive-looking. Deep = everything sharp — documentary, unsettling.Shallow depth of field is the single fastest upgrade to a cheap-looking shot.
LIGHTINGKey = the main light. Fill = the soft light that keeps shadows from going black. Rim = a light behind the subject that draws a bright edge around them. Never say "cinematic lighting" — say where the light is."A single lamp from the left, everything else dark" beats "moody lighting" every time.
ASPECT RATIOThe shape of the frame. 16:9 = normal screens and YouTube. 9:16 = vertical, TikTok/Reels/Shorts. 2.39:1 = very wide, cinema, feels serious. 1:1 = square.Pick one at the start. Mixing them is the fastest way to make a film look unfinished.
FOLEYEveryday physical sound: footsteps, a door, a rustle of clothes, glass breaking. Named after a man who recorded them for films by doing them.You get one sentence for foley. The single most effective sound in any film is usually a footstep on a specific surface.
CONTINUITYKeeping the world consistent: same face, same clothes, same hand holding the same thing, same side of the room, same light.The thing AI does worst and audiences notice most. Decide it once, write it down, repeat it every time.

Words that sound smart and mean nothing to the model

Do not say

  • cinematic · epic · stunning · beautiful · award-winning
  • emotional · dramatic tension · heartbreaking
  • masterpiece · best quality · 8k · ultra detailed

These are adjectives aimed at a human judge, not instructions to a camera.

Say instead

  • medium shot, low angle, slow dolly in
  • one warm practical lamp from the left, everything else black
  • she looks away, swallows, eyes stay on the table
  • shallow depth of field, 35mm, slight grain

Specific, filmable, and checkable. Every one of these is an instruction.

Ten mistakes every beginner makes

All ten are recoverable. None of them are stupid — they're what happens when you skip a step that only feels like it matters later.

Mistake 1

Jumping straight to video

You burn 40 credits and get beautiful nothing, because the composition was never decided. Fix: make the still first. Always. It costs seconds.

Mistake 2

Making a brand-new face every shot

The character is a different person in clip 1 and clip 4. Fix: one canon photo, uploaded every single time. No exceptions.

Mistake 3

Describing feelings instead of bodies

"She looks heartbroken" gives you a blank face. Fix: what do the eyes, jaw, throat and hands actually do?

Mistake 4

One action per long clip

You ask for "a person walking" and get a mannequin sliding, or a twitchy 0.5-second gesture. Fix: add weight and posture — a hand adjusting, a breath, a shift of weight.

Mistake 5

Every clip exactly the same length

Six four-second clips read as a slideshow no matter how good they are. Fix: vary the lengths. Let the important one be the longest.

Mistake 6

Forgetting the sound

Silent footage looks like a video game render, no matter how photoreal the picture is. Fix: one line about the room tone. That's all it takes.

Mistake 7

Asking for two camera moves at once

"Orbit while craning up and rack focusing" gives you confused camera motion. Fix: one move. Then a second prompt for the second move.

Mistake 8

Making a 90-second scene

Scope is what kills beginner projects. Nothing gets finished. Fix: 30 seconds, one location, one or two people. Then grow.

Mistake 9

Staring at one model

Every tool has a personality. The shot you want might be impossible in the one you're using. Fix: when stuck twice, switch tool rather than re-rolling 20 times.

Mistake 10

Not finishing

Forty beautiful clips and no edit. The edit is the film. Fix: set a hard limit — 20 clips, then assemble whatever you have.

The sentence that saves the most money

Ask this before every generation

"Have I decided this shot, or am I gambling on it?"

Re-rolling an undesigned shot is paying full price to be surprised. The tools are extraordinarily good at rendering a shot you designed. They are also extraordinarily good at rendering a shot you didn't.

Orientation

The biggest misconception in AI video

You do not type a prompt and get a film. You run a production. Everything in this document exists to close the gap between those two sentences.

“The biggest misconception about AI filmmaking in general is you type in a prompt and get a film. It’s artistry. It takes work to tell a meaningful story that resonates with other people. You can’t create an AI film that resonates with an audience without understanding how to craft an incredible story.”
— Caleb Ward, Co-Founder & CEO, Curious Refuge

Modern video models are not dumb pixel generators. They hold deep spatio-temporal priors for motion, weight, light and performance. They are, in effect, a skilled film crew that has never been given a script, a blocking plan, or a lighting plot — and has no memory of yesterday's take.

They fail for exactly one reason: ambiguity. Literary prose, swapped eyelines, dropped props, unnamed hands, drifting light. When a prompt contains two plausible readings, the model's visual reasoning picks one at random and then has to commit to it for every remaining frame.

Your job as the director is therefore not to be poetic. It is to eliminate ambiguity and set clean coordinate fences — while leaving the model room to do what it is genuinely good at.

The three failure archetypes

Archetype A

The Prompt Prayer

One paragraph of vibes → 8 seconds of beautiful nothing. Beautiful lighting, no story, no performance, no cut.

Fix: write a treatment and a shot list first. Never generate a shot you haven't designed.

Archetype B

The Continuity Collapse

Shot 4 looks nothing like shots 1–3. The character aged, the coat changed, the knife is in the other hand.

Fix: lock canon images, restate volatile physical state every beat, and clamp state at clip seams.

Archetype C

The Render Trap

You burn 40 credits per generation and 300 generations to get 12 usable seconds.

Fix: board in cheap image models, generate a motionless first frame, then animate a decided composition.

The one-line operating principle

Never let a video model make a decision you should have made. It is a brilliant cinematographer with perfect physical intuition and zero taste, zero memory, and no idea what happens next. Give it a decided frame and a coordinate fence, and it will outperform almost anything a human operator could rig.

The System

The 8-Stage Production Pipeline

Every stage has a deliverable. If a stage has no artifact you can point at, you are not finished with it.

00

Ideation & Story Engine

Use a text model as a writing room, not a script generator. Ask for loglines, wants, obstacles, reversals and what the character cannot say. Generate twenty loglines, keep three, throw away seventeen. A text model will give you a serviceable draft in ninety seconds — the skill is knowing that 95% of what it writes is unusable.

DeliverableLogline + treatment Time1–2 days
01

Pre-Production & Directorial Treatment

Before a single diffusion prompt, decide the emotional spine, the visual medium, the aspect ratio (16:9 vs 2.39:1 anamorphic), and the colour bible. Then break the script into continuous camera beats.

  • Script breakdown — convert prose into continuous camera beats, not disjointed paragraphs.
  • Colour bible — hard palette limits. Sodium-vapour amber, wet-asphalt cyan, pitch black. Three colours, not thirty.
  • Cinematography brief — lens language. Cooke Anamorphic 40mm, Panavision System 65, 1980s magnetic VHS.
  • Physical friction laws — surface contacts, gravity, fluid drag, prop behaviour.
DeliverableShot list + colour bible + lens brief Time1–3 days
02

Visual Anchoring — The Canon Bible

This is the stage everyone skips, and it is the reason everyone's film falls apart at cut three. Build locked still images for every character, environment and hero prop before you generate a single moving frame.

  • 4-angle turnaround sheets — front portrait, 3/4 profile, low-angle dynamic, micro-expression sheet.
  • Costume separation — identity comes from the casting tag; wardrobe is designed separately in the lookbook.
  • Frame 0 compositing — the opening frame of every take gets clean depth, correct eyelines, pre-loaded prop grips.
Director's pro tip

Never jump to video generation without locking master keyframes first. Pure text-to-video produces chaotic character drift and unpredictable geometry — and it is also the most expensive place to discover that problem.

DeliverableCharacter canon + location bible + Frame 0s Time2–4 days
03

Prompt Architecture

Now — and only now — you write prompts. At the floor level this is a shot brief in plain language. At the ceiling it is a full Auteur Script with five macro-blocks, chained sub-states and a locked opening frame.

DeliverableAuteur Script per shot Time2–5 days
04

The Generation Engine

Choose the model tier per shot, not per project. A talking head, a fight, a landscape flyover and a product turntable are four different jobs and should never share a model.

  • Ingredients / reference-to-video — up to 3 locked visual assets (Flow), or up to 50 tagged references (Seedance 2.5).
  • Frame-anchored generation — start frame + optional end frame to calculate plausible motion between two decided compositions.
  • Video-to-video & motion pre-vis — pass rehearsal footage through pose/depth conditioning to enforce real human mass.
DeliverableSelects + hero takes Time2–10 days
05

Diegetic Foley & Sound Design

Sound carries most of the immersion in a moving image. AI footage without tactile foley looks synthetic and detached, no matter how good the lighting is.

  • Persistent room tone — locked once in the lookbook, never re-typed per line.
  • Synchronised tactical foley — footsteps on gravel, metallic clangs, clothing rustle, glass shatter.
  • Voice — native model audio, or reference-audio cloning in an omni model.
  • Score — added in post. Never handed to the video engine.
DeliverableMixed stems Time1–3 days
06

Editorial Assembly

Assemble in DaVinci Resolve or Premiere. Cut on subject motion, not on clip boundaries. A good AI assembly breathes; a bad one feels like a slideshow of separately-generated clips.

  • Match on action — punch initiation cuts to impact landing, not before/after.
  • Rhythm design — vary shot duration. Three identical 4-second clips read as a slideshow.
  • Hidden cuts — insert a macro insert (a hand, a puddle, a flickering sign) to cover a visible seam.
DeliverableLocked rough cut Time1–3 days
07

Grade, Finish & Deliver

Unify the texture. Different models have different grain, contrast and highlight roll-off; a single grade pass is what makes them read as one film rather than a compilation.

  • Grain match — one consistent film stock across all sources.
  • Halation & bloom — a light pass on highlights unifies everything instantly.
  • Upscale — Topaz Video AI or a dedicated upscaler to a clean 4K master.
  • Deliver — platform-specific masters, plus a vertical 9:16 cut for social.
Deliverable4K master + platform cuts Time1–2 days

Budget reality

Generation cost is driven almost entirely by how many times you render the same shot, not by how many seconds the film is. A well-prepped shot takes 6–12 generations. A badly prepped shot takes 200 and still misses. Practise "draft cheap, commit expensive": board in a fast image model, animate in Lite/Fast tiers, promote only the frames you selected to the expensive tier.

Progression

The Skill Ladder

Most tutorials hand you level 3 and skip levels 1 and 2. Here is the actual path, in order, with the deliverable that proves you have reached each level.

L0Beginner

The Single Shot

Can do: generate one 6–8 second clip from a text description that reads as a real film shot — correct subject, correct camera language, correct ambience.

What you learn: that camera + subject + action + setting + light is a complete shot. That fewer, harder words beat more, softer words. That you must actually watch the output.

Tell: you describe lighting as "cinematic" or "moody". You're still asking instead of directing.

Proves level with: a 3-shot sequence where all three clips share a colour palette and a lens language.

L1Sequencer

Continuity Across Shots

Can do: keep one character recognisable across 10+ shots using a canon image and reference inputs. Build a shot list and generate it in order, not spontaneously.

What you learn: canon images, Ingredients/References, Frame 0, the 180° rule, complementary eyelines, and that a film's power is in cutting, not in any single shot.

Tell: you generate in whatever order is fun, then try to fix continuity in the edit. It can't be fixed there.

Proves level with: a 60–90 second sequence where an audience can't spot which model made which shot.

L2Director

Performance, Blocking & Sound

Can do: direct sustained compound performance, motivated camera movement inside a single take, tactile foley, and consistent voice. You start choosing models per shot.

What you learn: literal over literary, gaze-precedes-motion, asymmetric hands, the anti-twitch principle, diegetic purity, and the three consistent-voice workflows.

Tell: you still say "he looks sad" instead of describing what his body does when he's sad.

Proves level with: a 2-minute sequence with dialogue, a moving oner, and a sound design that survives a mute viewing.

L3Auteur

Architect of Reasoned Video

Can do: write a full Auteur Script — intent, logic fences, lookbook, locked opening frame, and a chained Sub-State timeline — and hand it to a reasoning-based model as a production document.

What you learn: the complete Auteur Prompting Standard, multi-clip state clamping, region edits, reference packs of 50 tagged assets, and multi-model pipelines.

Tell: you direct the model with coordinates, not adjectives.

Proves level with: a multi-sequence short where continuity survives a 5-clip seam, in two aspect ratios, with diegetic sound in every take.

The Directing Engine

The Auteur Prompting Standard

A modular cinematic prompt specification and pre-visualisation notation for reasoning-based generative video models. It replaces literary prose with explicit physical and optical coordinate fences.

Why a notation at all

Reasoning-based video models (Seedance 2.5, Veo 3.1, Kling, Runway) do not read prose literally. They run a visual-planning pass first: what is in frame, where, and what changes? Prose is ambiguous, so the planning pass guesses — and once it guesses, the whole clip is committed to that guess. A notation removes the guess.

The standard also separates staging (everything that is true before the first frame) from execution (everything that changes on screen). Mixing them is the single most common cause of drift: a model that is told a character's wardrobe in three places will try to satisfy all three and average them into something new.

The 4-Tier Conceptual Model

Tier 1

Macro-Block

A structural container on its own line, in square brackets. It partitions the script into an isolated creative responsibility.

[INTENT] [LOGIC] [AESTHETIC] [OPENING] [EXECUTION]

Tier 2

Macro-State

One line inside [EXECUTION]. A continuous beat interval — an unbroken stretch of screen time.

Consecutive lines may hold the same setup, or cut. Each begins from the visible reality the previous line left.

Tier 3

Sub-State

An atomic segment inside a Macro-State's -> chain. Modifies exactly one dimension of the frame.

This is the Single-Dimension Law: one tag, one job.

Tier 4

Coordinate Tag

The uppercase bracket that opens a Sub-State, telling the model which dimension is being directed.

[CAM] [ACT] · dialogue · <sound>

The Chaining Operator ->

Sub-states sequence left to right within a beat. This isn't a list of steps — these layers resolve into a single synchronised beat interval. That's the whole point. A camera move and a body move and a footstep happen at the same instant, not in sequence.

Canonical chain
[CAM] DOLLY IN -> [ACT] Arthur squeezes the fuel nozzle with his right hand, leans against the car chassis, and squints upward -> Arthur says (quietly) {You're late.} -> <hollow chrome pump tick>

The 5 Standard Macro-Blocks

[INTENT] — the deliverable brief + dramatic stakes

  • Written from the stance of director-producer at once. Begin with "Create …" as one flowing sentence.
  • Weaves format/medium, setting container, primary characters, and core stakes in prose.
  • No colon-separated labels. No shot-by-shot action. No plot synopsis.
  • Declare reference images only here: featuring @image1 as Mark and @image2 as Robert. Downstream blocks use canonical names only — never repeat @image tags.
  • Complete Costume Transformation: reference images establish facial identity and age only. Never copy their wardrobe. Design a new full-body costume in [AESTHETIC].

[LOGIC] — the non-negotiable physical fences

One dense, unpadded paragraph. Affirmative directive language: Ensure, Maintain, Preserve, Prioritise, Track, Anchor. It carries the 180° axis, screen direction, prop permanence, hand-specific allocations, grounded momentum, directional light behaviour, and match-on-action. Strictly diegetic physics — never meta-script instructions about prompt formatting or shot counts.

[AESTHETIC] — the master lookbook

Category: Value format, exactly one category per line, 4–7 lines typical, values compact and tactile. Build the location with specialised technical terminology — 304-grade brushed stainless steel pass-through tables, not a shiny kitchen. Name distinct key and rim sources. Wardrobe goes on one line, head to footwear. Audio here is the persistent room tone, and only here — never re-typed per line.

[OPENING] — the motionless first frame

Written by a DP and a gaffer capturing a frozen still photograph. 2–4 sentences, 40–70 words. No square-bracket tags. No motion verbs. No re-describing anything already in [AESTHETIC].

The 4 Essential Coordinates

  • Optical canvas — shot scale, angle, framing height.
  • Subject posture & set contact — frame-left/centre/right, FG/MG/BG, resting contact.
  • Prop allocations & hand grips — pre-loaded grips, guaranteed unbroken handoff to Line 1.
  • Eyeline heading & background depth — gaze vector, visible threats or silhouettes.

Forbidden in [OPENING]

  • Any motion verb: walks, turns, tracking forward, reaching.
  • Any bracket tag.
  • Off-screen characters or unfolding action.
  • Anything already declared in the lookbook.
  • Purple prose. It is a photograph, not a paragraph.

[EXECUTION] — the rolling timeline

Each line is one Macro-State and begins from the accumulated condition of the previous line, including the camera. Write in strict present tense, active voice, and anchor every physical action to an explicit subject.

Sub-State density

Minimum 3 per line. Most production scenes live at 3–8. A single- or double-tag line rarely gives the model enough physical scaffolding. Lean lines are fine for single-subject, low-risk beats — but if a prop transfers across a cut, two characters interact, or posture changes, go rich.

The Syntax Rules — Seedance 2.5 native

Correct
[CAM] MS, handheld, low-angle -> [ACT] John grips the box with his right hand -> John says (flatly) {Don't touch it.} -> <corrugated tear down the seam>
Wrong — every one of these is a real bug
[CAM: MS, handheld, low-angle]   // colon inside bracket
[ACT: gripping the box]            // unanchored participle
John says "Don't touch it."          // quotes, not braces
[AUDIO] sharp tape tear            // no such tag; use <sound>

  • Foley: <short physical sound description> — replaces baseline [AUDIO] tags entirely.

The Laws

These are not style preferences. Each one corresponds to a specific, documented failure mode.

Consistency is not a prompt trick. It is an asset-management problem. You build a bible of locked images first, then reference them everywhere. Here is the whole ladder.

The canon-image workflow

  1. Write the character bible before you generate a single pixel. Bone structure, skin undertone, hair, permanent scars and marks, age, build, and a one-line verbal rhythm. Vague anchors produce a different person every render; specific anchors produce the same person.
  2. Generate the character sheet. One subject, neutral lighting, plain background, three angles, neutral expression. Neutral is deliberate.
  3. Promote one frame to canon. Pick the single best front-facing image. Name it CHAR_NAME_canon_v1. Everything else is a supporting reference. Version it — v1, v2 — and never overwrite.
  4. Build expression and wardrobe sheets separately. These are downstream assets. They inherit the canon face but must never be used as the canon.
  5. Lock the location the same way. A location canon — one wide establishing image plus one eye-level detail — is what stops your room from rearranging itself between shots.

LoRA training — the pro method

This is Curious Refuge's documented workflow, and it is the right answer when a character appears in dozens of assets.

  1. Collect at least 10 varied reference images. Different angles, expressions, lighting, settings. Do not use images from the same photoshoot or in the same clothes. Variety is what teaches flexibility.
  2. Check the data before you train. Hair must not cover the face. The subject must be clearly and consistently visible. A face that is 30% obscured in half your set is a face the LoRA will learn to obscure.
  3. Train on fal.ai. Start it, wait 5–10 minutes, and use the time to plan the locations and actions you'll prompt for once it's ready.
  4. Save the link immediately. Copy the diffusers LoRa file link from the training history. Put it in a spreadsheet with the rest of your asset register. This is the single most common step people forget, and it means retraining.
  5. Run inference with tight settings. Scale 1, steps 28, guidance 3.5, 4 images per run, 16:9. Adjust guidance only after the first render.
  6. Upscale without face recovery. 6×, "High Fidelity", everything else at 1. Face recovery is the fastest route to the plastic, obviously-generated look.
The two data rules that actually matter

More reference images = better results, but quality and consistency beat quantity. Fifteen near-identical photos of the same pose teach a LoRa nothing. Ten genuinely varied images teach it a face. And keep the lighting roughly consistent even as angles and poses vary — you want the LoRA to learn identity, not a lighting setup.

Advanced: identity vs wardrobe vs lighting

At level 3 you stop treating a character image as a single object and start treating it as three separable layers you can recombine per shot:

  • Identity layer — the canon face. Locked. Never re-posed dramatically.
  • Wardrobe layer — designed per scene. This is where the Auteur Standard's Complete Costume Transformation rule earns its keep: a reference photo establishes the face, and the lookblock re-costumes the body from scratch.
  • Lighting layer — the lookbook's Lighting: line. Changed freely, because it was never baked into your canon.

Keep the three layers in separate files with separate filenames. The moment a character image contains all three fused, you can no longer change the coat without regenerating the face, and you will have re-shot a scene at 2am wondering why the actor's jawline moved.

How to build a reference pack that actually earns its slots

1 identity image (canon face) · 1 wardrobe plate · 1 location wide · 1 location detail · 1 motion reference video (a real camera move you want replicated) · 1 performance reference (an actor doing the gesture) · 1–2 audio references (room tone, voice). Tag every single one. An untagged reference is a coin flip.

Operating rules

Generation discipline

  • No negative prompts. Use positive constraint statements instead: "preserve identity", not "don't change the face".
  • Append a quality suffix to every prompt.
  • Start short, then extend. Confirm 5–10s, then extend. Never generate 30s to test a look.
  • Generate 2–4 variants per run and compare. This is cheaper than it sounds.
  • Re-check your @ tags. Multi-reference prompts are where mistakes happen most often.
  • Keep reference videos short. Trim to the key segment — shorter references give more precise results.
  • Prefer gentle motion words in the camera line: slow, gentle, continuous, natural, smooth.

Multi-shot control

  • Include the phrase "lens switch" to trigger a cut within a single generation. Characters and environment stay consistent across the cut.
  • One 15–30s clip can then cover multiple angles or beats instead of one continuous shot.
  • For an explicit multi-cut narrative with voice binding and per-shot control, Kling 3.0 remains an alternative worth testing — it advertises up to 6 cuts in narrative mode.
  • Whatever the model, describe the cut, not the montage. "lens switch" plus a clear new optical setup in the same sentence.

The routing principle

Mixing two or three models in one project is not a compromise — it is the professional default, and the only way to get the best of each. Your grade and grain pass is what makes the mix invisible. Without that pass, a multi-model project reads as a compilation. With it, it reads as a film shot on a weird stock.

When to add a sound tag — and when not to

Video models already infer obvious sounds from visible action. Adding a footstep tag to a character who is visibly walking is redundant noise. Deploy <sound> selectively, in exactly three cases:

Unique material friction

<wet boot squelch against clay>
<cardboard scraping on concrete>
<slick grease and water squeal against chef twill>

When the surface is doing the acting. Generic footstep on generic ground is already inferred.

Off-screen origin

<sudden floorboard creak in the hallway upstairs>

Acoustic events outside frame that the model cannot infer — and which should motivate a reaction.

Dramatic emphasis

<metallic revolver cylinder clicks shut>
<deafening bone-jarring impact clang>

Focal acoustic punctuation. The sound that makes an audience flinch.

The third case doubles as a directing device: an off-screen sound trigger is the cleanest way to make an actor react within a chained beat — <low wood-snapping vibration in canopy> -> [ACT] Rosa freezes and spins toward the sound.

Consistent voice — three workflows

Native audio is now standard, which creates a new problem: everyone wants the same character to sound like the same character across every shot. Curious Refuge documents three approaches, in ascending order of reliability.

Workflow 1

Native AI prompting

How: upload a starting frame and define the character's performance and dialogue in the text prompt. The model's internal engine generates both performance and voice.

  • Pros — simplest and fastest. No external audio tooling at all.
  • Cons — "metallic" artefacts, and vocal characteristics don't hold across shots. Better for stylised or deliberately over-the-top characters than for realistic, nuanced performance.
Workflow 2

Speech-to-speech & audio isolation

How: a technical post-production approach that overrides the native, inconsistent audio.

  1. Voice replacement — run the native audio through a speech-to-speech tool such as ElevenLabs Speech-to-Speech to replace it with a consistent cloned voice.
  2. Audio extraction — use an audio-isolation tool to pull the original room tone and effects out of the generated video's audio bed.
  3. Final mix — combine the new voice-over with the isolated background in your NDE, applying EQ and reverb so the new audio sits in the scene rather than sitting on it.

Pros — total control, works with any model. Cons — underused, multi-step, and the mix step is where most people get it wrong and lose all the atmosphere.

Workflow 3 — the recommended one

Reference audio cloning

How: provide an omni-capable model (Kling and similar) with a reference audio clip of the voice you want — ideally around 15 seconds. The model uses it to generate the same voice across subsequent shots.

Pro tip — give it tonal range

Supply varied reference clips: ~15 seconds of the character sounding sad, then ~15 seconds sounding excited. The model needs to understand the character's full tonal range, not just one mood, or every shot lands in the same emotional register.

Pros — highest consistency without manual ADR or complex external mixing. Cons — still not 100% identical to a live actor, but it is currently the best balance of quality and effort.

Delivery

Assembly, Grade & Finish

This is where separate clips become one film. It is also the cheapest stage to fix problems and the stage most beginners under-use.

Editorial rules

Cut on motion

  • Match on action — cut on the punch initiation, so the next shot opens on the impact landing. Never cut before-and-after the action; cut through it.
  • Cut on gaze — leave a shot while the character is still looking at where the next shot will be.
  • Cut on sound — a footstep starting in shot A and completing in shot B is the oldest trick in the book and still works.

Design rhythm

  • Vary shot duration. Three identical four-second clips read as a slideshow regardless of how good they are.
  • Let the best take be the longest. Don't cut away from a great performance because a clip is "too long".
  • Build to a change. The shot before the story turns should be the one that makes you look twice.

Hide the seams

  • Insert a macro. A hand, a puddle, a flickering sign, a passing head — two frames of a cutaway covers any continuity error.
  • Cut on darkness. Move through a shadow or a light flare between takes; it's a free dissolve.
  • Change the frame. If two adjacent clips share an identical composition, reframe one slightly in post.

Don't do this

  • Don't cut on the clip boundary. That is the "AI video" signature.
  • Don't grade each clip separately. Grade the timeline.
  • Don't add music to fix pacing. Fix the pacing.
  • Don't let a clip's native room tone fight another clip's — normalise it first.

Colour & texture unification

Different models have genuinely different physics of light. One grade pass is the single highest-leverage thing you can do to make a multi-model project cohere.

  1. Normalise first. Log-match exposure and white balance across all clips before you look at anything creative. Use scopes, not your eyes.
  2. Pick one stock. Apply a single film-stock emulation to the entire timeline — never per clip. This alone fixes most "compilation" tells.
  3. Bloom and halation. A light pass on the highlights, slightly out of register, unifies everything instantly and is how film actually behaves.
  4. Grade in one node tree. One contrast curve, one palette limit — the three colours from your colour bible and nothing else.
  5. Match grain to shot size. Grain should be finer in close-ups and coarser in wides. A single flat grain over mixed shot sizes is a dead giveaway.

Finishing

  • Upscale to a clean 4K master with a dedicated video upscaler. Avoid aggressive face recovery — it reintroduces the plastic look at the exact moment you were trying to remove it.
  • Audio post happens last and gets the most time. Dialogue clarity, then foley, then ambience, then a thin score bed. If the mix doesn't survive a mute viewing, it isn't finished.
  • Deliver platform-specific masters. A 2.39:1 feature master, a 16:9 web master, and a 9:16 vertical cut with re-composed framing — not a cropped centre.

The most expensive habit in AI filmmaking

Re-rolling a prompt that describes the wrong shot. If a take misses, the first question is never "what word should I change" — it is "was the shot actually designed?" Re-rolling an undesigned shot is paying full price to sample from a distribution you didn't specify.

The 20 rules

  1. Never let the model make a decision you should have made.
  2. Lock the canon before you lock the shot.
  3. Lock the first frame before you move a single pixel of it.
  4. Reference images carry identity only. Never wardrobe.
  5. Staging before execution. Zero motion verbs in the opening frame.
  6. One tag, one dimension. The Single-Dimension Law.
  7. Sub-states resolve simultaneously. They are not a sequence of steps.
  8. Never end a beat on dialogue.
  9. 180° is not negotiable. Complementary eyelines or nothing.
  10. Screen coordinates are geography. Restate them.
  11. Restate hand-specific grips on every relevant beat.
  12. Every physical condition persists until something mutates it.
  13. Direct the body, never the emotion. Literal over literary.
  14. Compound your actions. One twitch is a mannequin.
  15. Nobody visible stands still. Acting is reacting.
  16. Give the hands different jobs.
  17. Eye first, head second, torso third.
  18. Room tone once, in the lookbook. Foley per beat. Score in post.
  19. Name the material, not the sound.
  20. Choose the model per shot, not per project.

First 30 days

Week 1

One shot a day

Use the Level 0 formula. Vary only the camera move. Watch every output at full speed, then mute, then half speed. Don't read about tools; generate in them.

Week 2

One character, ten shots

Build a canon sheet. Then generate ten shots of the same person in the same location. The goal is one flawless identity, not ten pretty images.

Week 3

One 60-second sequence

Storyboard it, lock every Frame 0, generate in order, assemble in SceneBuilder. Fix the sound. This is your first real piece of work.

Week 4

One Auteur Script

Take that 60 seconds and rewrite it as a full five-block Auteur Script. Regenerate it. Compare the two. That gap is your entire learning curve, in one exercise.
